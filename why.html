1 · The slow leak: inflation

$100 in 1945 buys $5.63 of goods today.

Cash doesn't sit still. Since the end of the Second World War, every major currency has lost most of its purchasing power: the US dollar at an average of 3.7% a year, the British pound at 4.8%, the Japanese yen at 5.7%. Even the Swiss franc, the most disciplined of them all, lost more than 80% of its value.

This isn't an accident. Since the 1990s most central banks have explicitly targeted positive inflation, typically 2% a year, and the bursts above target (1947, the 1970s, 2022) come on top of that.

Savers who hold Treasury bills or money-market funds have historically roughly kept pace with inflation. The loss below is what happens to money held as cash: in a wallet or a checking account, which is exactly where spending money lives.

      2 · The sudden break: bank failures

      Bank failures come in waves, not a trickle.

      A bank balance isn't cash in a vault. Legally it's a loan to the bank, and it's only as safe as the bank that owes it to you. In the United States, the one country that counts failures precisely, more than 4,100 banks have failed or needed rescue since 1934.

      The failures cluster. Three episodes account for most of the assets lost since the Depression: the savings and loan crisis, the 2008–12 financial crisis and the 2023 regional bank failures. Long quiet stretches, like 1943–74, end abruptly. Every economy in the chart below has had at least one wave.

      Deposit insurance covers most individual balances in most crises, but it has limits and it isn't instant. When a bank fails, getting access to your money depends on a regulator's timetable, not yours.

      3 · The switch: frozen funds

      If someone else holds your money, someone else can freeze it.

      Getting to money in a bank depends on a chain of intermediaries: banks, card networks, payment processors and fundraising platforms. Any one of them can be ordered to stop the money moving, or can decide to do so on its own.

      The cases below differ widely in cause, context and severity. Some were ordered by governments, some were private decisions, and some were whole banking systems shutting their doors in a crisis. In several, courts later ruled the freezes unlawful or ordered them reversed. The cause varies. The mechanism doesn't: when your money exists only as an entry on someone else's books, whoever controls those books controls your access to it.

      Incidents by type, 1933–2026

      Dots mark a single year and bars mark a period. Hover a mark for a summary, or click it to jump to the details.

      What counts

      An incident is included when a person's or group's access to money held with an intermediary was blocked or cut. That covers blocking by order, under pressure, or in a systemic crisis. Sanctions between states and ordinary criminal asset seizures are left out.

      4 · Why the 3F card

      Keep your wealth. Spend your budget.

      The three problems above share one cause: money held as a balance on someone else's books. It loses value while it sits there, it's only as safe as the institution holding it, and access to it can be switched off.

      The 3F card is designed for people who keep most of their wealth in their own custody, in wallets only they control, and who want to spend it in everyday life without handing it to a bank first.

      1

      Self-custody your wealth

      Your long-term holdings stay in your own wallet. 3F never holds them, and no bank balance stands between you and your savings.

      2

      Fund a monthly budget

      On your schedule, you move the crypto you plan to spend into your 3F smart contract. Only your monthly budget is ever exposed to any intermediary, whether the card network or the stablecoin issuer. Everything else stays in your own custody.

      3

      Spend anywhere Visa is accepted

      When you use the card, crypto from your budget is converted to a stablecoin (USDC or USDT) and paid to the merchant over the Visa network. There's no bank account in the middle.

      4

      Pay less tax on every purchase

      Every crypto purchase is a taxable sale. Our tax optimization engine picks which lots to sell, following IRS capital gains rules, to keep your tax bill as low as possible.
